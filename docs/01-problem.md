# 01 — The problem

## Why this group, why now

This project is an unusual messenger, and that matters.

The maintainers and contributors are not only users of mass spectrometry
data. They are also builders of high-fidelity simulators and
synthetic-data workflows, including TimSim, Synthedia, SMITER, and
related tooling. That gives them both the means and the obligation to
sign simulated output first.

> We build the simulators. We are among the first people obligated to
> sign our output, because we are among the first with the means to
> create synthetic data that can be mistaken for real acquisitions at
> scale. Here is what that looks like. Now we would like to talk to
> vendors about the other half.

Shipping a working simulator-side reference implementation from tools we
control is the most credible opening move in this initiative. It shows
the principle in working code before asking vendors, repositories,
converters, or standards bodies to adopt the broader chain.

---

## The problem

Mass spectrometry data formats — vendor RAW and derived formats like
mzML — lack cryptographic guarantees of authenticity, integrity, and
provenance.

Users cannot reliably distinguish between:

- Real experimental data acquired on an instrument
- Simulated data generated by software
- Data that has been modified or partially fabricated

High-fidelity simulators (TimSim, Synthedia, SMITER) and generative
models have closed the inspection gap. Synthetic mass spectra are
already indistinguishable from real acquisitions by eye, and the
problem will only grow.

This matters for:

- Reproducibility of published results
- Benchmarking of search engines and pipelines
- Scientific trust and peer review
- Clinical and regulatory applications (21 CFR Part 11, IVDR)

---

## Core question

> How can a user verify that a dataset (RAW or mzML) originates from a
> real instrument, has not been tampered with, and — if derived —
> faithfully descends from a specific acquisition?

---

## Current limitations

**RAW (vendor formats).** No signatures, no tamper detection, no
verifiable origin.

**mzML.** Fully editable. No enforced link to RAW. The
`dataProcessing` and `softwareList` slots record transformation
history informationally — not signed, not verifiable. The optional
`<fileChecksum>` element is a self-checksum (SHA-1, computed by the
same software that wrote the file) and provides no defense against an
attacker who recomputes it after tampering. See [`faq.md`](faq.md) for
why this is not equivalent to a cryptographic provenance signature.

**Repositories.** Accept submissions on trust. No cryptographic binding
between uploader, dataset, and acquisition.

---

## Trust gaps

| Gap | Description |
|-----|------------|
| Origin | No proof data comes from a real instrument |
| Integrity | No detection of modifications |
| Lineage | No verifiable RAW → mzML transformation |
| Authenticity | Simulated or forged data indistinguishable from real |
| Submission | No binding between dataset, submitter, and repository |

mzprov addresses **integrity** and **authenticity** end-to-end for
simulator/converter output today. **Origin**, **lineage**, and
**submission** are addressed by the parallel tracks of the long-term
deployment plan — see [`02-architecture.md`](02-architecture.md) and
[`05-roadmap.md`](05-roadmap.md).

---

## One-line summary

> Establish a cryptographically verifiable chain of custody for mass
> spectrometry data, anchored incrementally at the simulator, the
> repository, and ultimately the instrument, enabling users to
> distinguish authentic data from simulated or manipulated datasets.
